# Round 2 — households that want different things

*Does the result survive a town where people are not all the same?*

## The objection this round was built to answer

Round 1 found that credit makes financed goods dearer and harder to get for the household that never borrows. But in round 1 everyone in the town essentially wanted the same things in the same proportions. Some had more money than others, and some were keener shoppers than others, but nobody preferred good food to a new television, or durability to style.

Real towns are not like that. And if the whole effect depended on everyone chasing the same washing machine at the same moment, it would be an artefact of a modelling shortcut rather than a finding.

So round 2 gave the town four kinds of household. Everything else — the six categories, the thousand households, the incomes, the fixed supply — stayed exactly as it was, so that anything that changed can only be the people.

## The four kinds of household

| Type | Share of the town | What they are like |
|---|---|---|
| **The thrifty** | 30 % | Want less of everything and care least about quality anywhere |
| **The health-conscious** | 20 % | Spend on good food rather than more food; less interested in gadgets |
| **The gadget lovers** | 20 % | Want far more hobby and electronics — and want them good |
| **The practical family** | 30 % | More clothing and appliances, chosen for durability rather than finish |

Two separate things vary between them: **how much** they want a category at all, and **how far up** the quality ladder they climb when they buy. Those are genuinely different traits — "buys the best food, but only one portion of it" is a real kind of household, and round 1 could not express it.

## The main result

| | round 1 (everyone alike) | round 2 (four types) |
|---|---|---|
| Share of what they wanted that the non-borrower got, without credit | 76.0 % | 78.2 % |
| …with credit | 71.7 % | 75.8 % |
| **Change** | **−5.6 %** | **−3.1 %** |

**The effect survives, but it halves.**

That is the honest headline of this round, and it is worth sitting with rather than explaining away: a more realistic population produces a smaller effect. Not a vanished one — it is still far larger than the run-to-run noise, comfortably enough to be sure of — but half the size.

## The price effect does not move at all

| | round 1 | round 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Electronics | **+11.0 %** | **+11.7 %** |
| Appliances | +7.6 % | +6.6 % |
| Clothing | −3.6 % | −1.5 % |
| All prices together | +0.5 % | +0.1 % |

Round 2's own two runs, side by side. Prices are an index: **1.00 is the price the good was first put on the shelf for**, so 1.34 means it is changing hands a third above that price and 0.86 means it is going for 14 % less.

| | without credit | with credit | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| **All prices together** | 1.032 | 1.033 | **+0.1 %** |
| Electronics | 0.962 | 1.075 | **+11.7 %** |
| Hobby | 0.864 | 0.923 | **+6.9 %** |
| Appliances | 1.344 | 1.432 | **+6.6 %** |
| Leisure | 0.955 | 0.954 | −0.1 % |
| Food | 1.133 | 1.118 | **−1.3 %** |
| Clothing | 0.922 | 0.908 | **−1.5 %** |

**The first column is not credit's doing.** Appliances already trade a third above their shelf price in the run where borrowing is impossible — four types of household all wanting appliances at once will do that on their own. Only the last column belongs to credit, and it is the same column in both rounds.

This is the sharper half of the result. **The two halves of the original claim behave differently.**

> *Does the good become more expensive for the person who never borrows?* — Yes, and just as much as before.
>
> *Does it become harder for them to get?* — Yes, but about half as much.

Both are still true. One of them is on much firmer ground.

## How much anyone actually consumes

Round 1's non-borrower bought fewer things under credit. Round 2's does not. Per household, per year:

| The non-borrower buys | without credit | with credit | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 11.77 | 11.80 | +0.2 % |
| Leisure | 11.81 | 11.82 | +0.1 % |
| Clothing | 1.87 | 1.88 | +0.8 % |
| Hobby | 0.906 | 0.883 | −2.6 % |
| Electronics | 0.282 | 0.266 | **−5.6 %** |
| Appliances | 0.0905 | 0.0866 | **−4.3 %** |
| **Everything, together** | **26.73** | **26.74** | +0.0 % |

The durables, as the gap between one purchase and the next:

| The non-borrower replaces… | without credit | with credit |
|---|---|---|
| an appliance every | 11.1 years | **11.6 years** |
| an electronic device every | 3.6 years | **3.8 years** |

And the town as a whole:

| Per household, per year | without credit | with credit | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everything the town consumed | 26.73 | 26.73 | **±0.0 %** |
| The same, weighted by quality | 25.40 | 25.40 | **±0.0 %** |
| What a borrower consumed | 26.73 | 26.73 | ±0.0 % |
| Their electronics | 0.281 | 0.282 | +0.5 % |
| Their appliances | 0.0906 | 0.0893 | −1.4 % |

**The town's total consumption does not move by a measurable amount** — which is the same conclusion round 1 reached, arrived at more cleanly. Round 1 lost a third of a per cent of its units; here the total is flat to three decimal places and the movement is entirely in who buys what. Credit does not add goods to the town and it does not destroy them. It re-sorts them.

The re-sorting is still visible in the two categories credit reaches. The non-borrower's electronics and appliances fall; the borrower's do not. Everything else the non-borrower buys goes very slightly **up**, which is the money they no longer spend on the appliance turning up somewhere else in the basket.

## Why the access effect shrank: there is less borrowing

| | credit outstanding in the town |
|---|---|
| Round 1 | €70,970 |
| Round 2 | €49,037 |

**A quarter to a third less credit is taken out — at exactly the same willingness to borrow.** Nothing about the credit terms changed.

The reason is that deciding to finance something is a *threshold*: either the purchase makes sense once you account for the instalments, or it does not. Spreading people out around that threshold pushes some over it and some back under it — and it does not push equal numbers each way. Fewer people end up financing. Less borrowing means less bidding, and the non-borrower feels proportionally less of it.

That is something a town of identical households simply cannot say.

## The objection I wrote down before running it

There is an obvious way this result could be an illusion, and it was worth naming in advance rather than being caught by it afterwards.

The four types above care *less* about quality on average than round 1's household did. A town that is more budget-minded to begin with might put more people on the cheap shelves before credit is even mentioned — which could produce the whole shrinkage without any real "different people want different things" effect at all.

So a fifth version of the town was written down in advance: identical to round 2, except that the quality preferences were rescaled to average out at exactly round 1's level. If the shrinkage came from the town being more budget-minded, this version would undo it.

| | change for the non-borrower |
|---|---|
| Round 2 | −3.083 % |
| Round 2, budget-mindedness rescaled away | −3.080 % |

**It moves the answer by three thousandths of a per cent.** It is not that the town got thriftier. It is that the people in it became different from one another.

## Something new, and untested

Under round 1's identical households, the quality of what the non-borrower bought did not measurably change under credit. With four types, it does — it goes slightly **up** (+0.3 %), while the *number* of things they buy stays flat.

They get about as many things, and those things are marginally better on average.

That is consistent with the idea that the purchases they lose are the cheap marginal ones — the things they only just wanted — while the expensive ones they were determined to have survive. **But that reading has not been tested**, and it should not be quoted as though it had. It is an observation looking for its own experiment.

## Conclusion

**The result is not an artefact of everyone being the same.** That was the live risk going into this round, and it is now measured rather than hoped: give the town four genuinely different kinds of household and the effect is still clearly there.

**But it is half the size, and that matters for how the claim should be stated.** The version of this argument that survives a realistic population is:

> The good that B never borrowed for becomes **exactly as much more expensive**, and about **half as much harder to get**.

Anyone writing this up should lead with the price and follow with access — not because access is untrue, but because it is the half that moved when the assumptions got more realistic, and it may well move again.

**And a caveat that applies to this whole round.** These four types are a guess about what a population looks like. Nothing in this project can prove they are the right four, and no amount of running the model will make them right. They were written down before any of them was run, and they were not adjusted afterwards to improve the answer — but they remain an assumption.

The next objection is more concrete, and it has a number attached: "electronics" is not something anyone owns. People own a phone, a laptop and a television, on three completely different rhythms and at three completely different prices. That is what round 3 was built to take apart.
