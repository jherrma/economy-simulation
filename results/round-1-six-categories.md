# Round 1 — six categories

*What happens to prices when part of the town buys on credit, and part of it never does.*

## What was tested

A small town of a thousand households. Everyone gets the same modest income every month — around €650 of spending money after rent, transport and insurance — though some get noticeably more and some noticeably less. Each month the shops receive a fixed number of things to sell. Nobody moves in or out, and there is no way to buy from outside the town.

The town was run twice. In the first run, **nobody can buy on credit**. In the second, everybody can — except for a fifth of households who never do, in either run. Those are the same households both times, with the same incomes and the same tastes. **What happens to them is the whole point.**

Every number below is the average over thirty pairs of runs. A single run tells you nothing here; the town is chaotic enough that one cent's difference at the start sends it somewhere else entirely.

## The town

| | |
|---|---|
| Households | 1,000 |
| Spending money per month | around €650 each |
| Categories of goods | 6 — food, leisure, clothing, hobby, electronics, appliances |
| Qualities per category | 3 — budget, standard, premium |
| Bought on credit? | electronics, appliances and hobby only |
| Households who never borrow | 200 |

## What happened to prices

Prices are shown as an index. **1.00 means a good is trading at exactly the price it was first put on the shelf for.** 1.18 means it is trading 18 % above that price; 0.95 means 5 % below it.

| | without credit | with credit | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| **All prices together** | 1.043 | 1.049 | **+0.5 %** |
| Electronics | 0.990 | 1.099 | **+11.0 %** |
| Appliances | 1.178 | 1.267 | **+7.6 %** |
| Hobby | 0.911 | 0.963 | **+5.8 %** |
| Food | 1.153 | 1.154 | +0.0 % |
| Leisure | 0.945 | 0.944 | −0.1 % |
| Clothing | 0.946 | 0.912 | **−3.6 %** |

**Read the last column, not the first two.** The levels are a property of this town rather than of credit: appliances trade 18 % above their shelf price even in the run where nobody can borrow at all, simply because more households want one each month than the factory makes. Both runs start from the same shelf prices, so the only thing credit can be blamed for is the *difference* between the two columns.

**The overall price level barely moves.** That is the first surprise, and it matters: if you only looked at an inflation figure, you would conclude credit does nothing at all.

What moves is *which* things are expensive. The three categories people finance become markedly dearer. Clothing — which nobody buys on instalments — becomes **cheaper**. Money that used to be spread across the shops is now concentrated on the shelves credit reaches.

## What happened to the household that never borrows

| | without credit | with credit | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share of everything they wanted that they got | 76.0 % | 71.7 % | **−5.6 %** |
| …of the electronics they wanted | 12.2 % | 8.0 % | **−33.9 %** |
| …of the appliances they wanted | 2.6 % | 2.0 % | **−24.1 %** |

A household that never took out a loan, whose income did not change and whose tastes did not change, now gets only **two-thirds as much of the electronics it wanted, and three-quarters as much of the appliances** — because other people financed theirs.

These are shares of what was wanted, and a want that goes unmet stays on the list until it is met, so the denominator grows as the household falls behind. That is a real thing happening to them, but it makes the percentage larger than the drop in what they actually carry home. The next section gives the plainer number.

## How much they actually consume

Counting the things themselves, per household, per year:

| The non-borrower buys | without credit | with credit | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Food | 11.61 | 11.57 | −0.3 % |
| Leisure | 11.79 | 11.79 | −0.1 % |
| Clothing | 1.83 | 1.85 | +0.9 % |
| Hobby | 0.88 | 0.85 | −4.3 % |
| Electronics | 0.277 | 0.252 | **−9.1 %** |
| Appliances | 0.090 | 0.082 | **−8.3 %** |
| **Everything, together** | **26.5** | **26.4** | −0.4 % |

The durables are easier to picture as the gap between one purchase and the next:

| The non-borrower replaces… | without credit | with credit |
|---|---|---|
| an appliance every | 11.1 years | **12.2 years** |
| an electronic device every | 3.6 years | **4.0 years** |

**Both measures are right, and they disagree because they answer different questions.** The share fell by a third; the count fell by a twelfth. The household buys nearly as many things — it just spends longer waiting for each of the expensive ones, and the wanting piles up while it waits.

And here is the same count set against the households that do borrow:

| Bought per household per year | non-borrower | borrower |
|---|---|---|
| Electronics, without credit | 0.277 | 0.279 |
| Electronics, with credit | **0.252** | 0.277 |
| Appliances, without credit | 0.090 | 0.090 |
| Appliances, with credit | **0.082** | 0.089 |

**Without credit the two are indistinguishable — as they should be, since nothing separates them but a habit nobody can act on.** Switch credit on and only one of them loses ground.

## What they bought instead

Priced out of the good version, they took the cheap version.

| Within each category, the non-borrower's share of… | budget goods | premium goods |
|---|---|---|
| Appliances | **+11.7 points** | −4.0 points |
| Electronics | **+7.1 points** | −3.6 points |
| Hobby | +1.8 points | −0.8 points |
| Food | −1.5 points | +0.7 points |
| Leisure | −0.4 points | +0.3 points |

They trade down where credit bids against them, and trade slightly *up* in food and leisure with the money left over. It is not that they became poorer — their income is identical. They were outbid.

## Nobody ends up with more

Per household, per year, across the whole town:

| | without credit | with credit | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everything the town consumed | 26.47 | 26.38 | **−0.4 %** |
| The same, weighted by quality | 25.11 | 25.01 | **−0.4 %** |
| What a borrower consumed | 26.47 | 26.38 | −0.4 % |
| The same, weighted by quality | 25.09 | 24.96 | −0.5 % |

**Credit creates no goods.** Once the town has settled down, the borrowers are not ahead either: their head start was taken years earlier and what is left of it is the monthly instalment. What credit changes is *when* things are bought and *who* gets them — never how many exist.

## How much credit does this take?

Less than you would guess.

| | |
|---|---|
| New lending, as a share of what the town spends on durables | **10 %** |
| Instalments, as a share of all spending | 1.3 % |
| Interest, as a share of all spending | 0.2 % |

A tenth of durable spending being financed is enough to move the non-borrower's access to appliances by a quarter. The effect does not come from the *volume* of credit. It comes from where it lands: on the handful of expensive things that are already hard to afford, where a small number of extra bidders decides the price.

## Does it depend on the interest rate?

Yes, and in the direction you would expect — cheaper credit means more of it, and more harm.

| Effective interest rate | non-borrower's appliances | non-borrower's electronics | electronics price |
|---|---|---|---|
| ≈ 13 % (typical card or BNPL) | **−26.0 %** | **−35.8 %** | **+11.6 %** |
| ≈ 15 % *(the rate used above)* | −24.5 % | −34.3 % | +11.0 % |
| ≈ 25 % | −15.2 % | −26.2 % | +7.7 % |
| ≈ 38 % | −9.6 % | −14.6 % | +3.9 % |

This one table is averaged slightly differently from the rest of the page — every row pools the thirty runs before taking the ratio, rather than taking the ratio run by run and averaging that. It has to be, because the four rows must be comparable with each other. That is why its middle row reads −24.5 % and −34.3 % where the tables above read −24.1 % and −33.9 %: identical runs, third-decimal difference, no disagreement about anything.

The rate used for the headline figures sits at the **cautious** end of the realistic range. At the rate a typical credit card or buy-now-pay-later offer actually charges, the measured harm is slightly *larger* than reported above.

## Conclusion

Three things came out of this round, and only the first was expected.

**One: the claim holds, in the direction it was meant to.** The goods people buy on instalments become more expensive, and the household that never borrows gets fewer of them. It ends up with a twelfth fewer electronic devices and gets only two-thirds of the ones it wanted — waiting four years for a replacement instead of three and a half.

**Two: "credit makes everything more expensive" is the wrong headline.** The overall price level moves by half a per cent. Anyone looking for this effect in an inflation figure would miss it completely. What credit does is *rearrange* which things are expensive: sharply up where it is used, slightly down where it is not.

**Three: nobody comes out ahead.** The town consumes slightly less in total, and the borrowers are no better off in the long run either. The whole effect is a transfer — from the person who does not finance, to the person who does, at the moment they bid for the same washing machine.

The obvious objection to this round is that everybody in this town wants roughly the same things. If the effect depends on the whole town chasing the same washing machine in the same month, it is a quirk of the model rather than a finding. That objection is what round 2 was built to answer.
