# Round 3 — eighteen goods

*Which things get harder to buy, for the household that never borrows — and why the answer is not what it looks like.*

## What changed since round 2

Rounds 1 and 2 treated "electronics" as a single good. Nobody owns electronics. People own a phone they replace every two and a half years, a laptop every four and a half, and a television every seven — three different sums of money on three different rhythms, and credit's whole job is bridging exactly that kind of lump.

So the town was rebuilt with **eighteen distinct goods** instead of six categories, each with its own price and its own replacement cycle. Round 2's four kinds of household were kept, and given one more difference: how often each type replaces a given thing. The gadget lover churns a phone every twenty months; the thrifty household keeps one for three and a half years.

The town also had to grow to five thousand households. With one thousand, some shelves held a single large washing machine per month, and a price taken from one item a month is not a measurement of anything.

Everything else is as before: fixed incomes, a fixed number of goods produced each month, one run where nobody can borrow and one where everybody can except the fifth who never do. Same households, same incomes, both times.

## The town

| | |
|---|---|
| Households | 5,000 |
| Spending money per month | around €650 each |
| Distinct goods | 18, in 6 categories |
| Qualities per good | 3 — budget, standard, premium |
| Households who never borrow | 1,000 |
| Months simulated | 100 years, of which the last 30 are measured |

## The main result

| | without credit | with credit | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share of what they wanted that the non-borrower got | 80.1 % | 76.7 % | **−4.2 %** |
| All prices together | 1.019 | 1.021 | **+0.1 %** |

The headline survives being rebuilt from scratch, and lands between round 1's −5.6 % and round 2's −3.1 %. And once again, **the overall price level says nothing at all**.

### How to read a price in this town

Every price below is an index. **1.00 means the good is changing hands at exactly the price it was first put on the shelf for.** 1.17 means it is going for 17 % more than that; 0.82 means it is going for 18 % less.

The levels are worth a moment, because they are not credit's doing and it would be easy to read them as if they were. This town has fixed incomes and a fixed number of goods each month, so prices settle wherever demand and the shelf meet — and that is a different place for every good, in both runs. The large washing machine is the clearest case:

| Large washing machine, €1,440 on the shelf | index | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| Without credit | **0.82** | It sells for 18 % *below* list. Almost nobody can put €1,440 together at once, so the shop has to discount to move any at all. |
| With credit | **1.14** | It sells for 14 % *above* list. Once the price can be split into instalments, enough households can reach it that they bid against each other. |

**Only the change between the two runs belongs to credit.** Both runs begin from the same shelf prices, with the same households and the same incomes; the only difference is whether the shop offers to split the payment. So read the change column throughout — a level of 1.23 for groceries says something about groceries in this town, not something about lending.

## Where the harm actually lands

This is what the round was built to find out: does credit hurt the non-borrower on the frequent, medium-sized purchase — or on the rare, enormous one?

| Good | price | replaced every | on credit? | share of it the non-borrower gets | its price |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Washing machine, large | €1,440 | 12 years | **yes** | **−58.3 %** | **+38.4 %** |
| Laptop | €900 | 4.5 years | yes | −32.8 % | +9.6 % |
| Smartphone | €600 | 2.5 years | yes | −27.6 % | +6.9 % |
| Television | €700 | 7 years | yes | −17.1 % | +2.8 % |
| Washing machine, medium | €640 | 8 years | yes | −16.8 % | +1.1 % |
| Large hobby kit | €540 | 5 years | yes | −7.2 % | +0.5 % |
| Hobby equipment | €216 | 18 months | yes | no measurable change | +0.4 % |
| Small appliances | €120 | 3 years | **no** | +2.1 % | −1.4 % |
| **Winter coat** | **€576** | **2 years** | **no** | **+16.0 %** | **−3.6 %** |
| Holiday | €360 | yearly | no | +4.3 % | −2.3 % |
| Everyday clothing | €152 | 8 months | no | +2.6 % | −1.5 % |
| Groceries | €210 | monthly | no | +0.1 % | −0.6 % |

**It is the rare, enormous one, and the gradient is steep.** The washing machine is hit more than twice as hard as the laptop and eight times harder than the large hobby kit.

That middle column is the share of what they wanted that they got, and an unmet want stays on the list until it is met — so the figure grows as the household falls further behind. It is the right measure of how badly they are being kept from a thing. It is not the number of machines. That is the next table.

Read the bottom half of the table too. Everything cheap and frequent moves the *other* way. The household that never borrows ends up with **more** coats, more holidays, more everyday clothing — and all of it slightly cheaper.

They are not simply worse off. They are pushed out of the top of their shopping basket and left marginally better off at the bottom of it.

## The result that settles the argument

Look at two goods in that table side by side.

| | price | replaced every | can be financed | share of it the non-borrower gets | its price |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Smartphone | €600 | 30 months | **yes** | **−27.6 %** | **+6.9 %** |
| Winter coat | €576 | 24 months | **no** | **+16.0 %** | **−3.6 %** |

Four per cent apart in price. Six months apart in replacement cycle. As far as this town is concerned they are the same purchase — a mid-sized thing you replace every couple of years.

**And the effect reverses sign.** The non-borrower gets a quarter less of the phones it wanted and a sixth more of the coats. The phone gets dearer; the coat gets cheaper.

The only difference between them is whether the shop offers to split the payment.

So it is **not that expensive things are hard to get.** Everybody already knew that expensive things are hard to get if you have little money — that is not a finding, it is a definition. What this says is different: **it is the things that can be financed that become hard to get, for the person who doesn't finance them.**

Without credit the two households are the same household in every respect that matters. With it, one waits three years longer and the other waits four months less.

## What the town consumes altogether

| Per household, per year | without credit | with credit | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everything the town consumed | 55.18 | 55.17 | **−0.02 %** |
| The same, weighted by quality | 52.49 | 52.48 | **−0.02 %** |
| Large washing machines | 0.0712 | 0.0703 | −1.3 % |
| Smartphones | 0.370 | 0.366 | −1.0 % |
| Laptops | 0.203 | 0.201 | −0.9 % |
| Televisions | 0.126 | 0.125 | −1.1 % |

**The town's total consumption does not move.** Fifty-five things per household per year, before and after, of the same average quality. Whatever credit is doing, it is not making the town richer or poorer in goods — there is no room for it to, because the factories make the same number of things either way.

What it does is decide who gets them. The town buys 1.3 % fewer large washing machines overall, and the non-borrowers absorb far more than their share of that: they take 17 % fewer while the borrowers take 3 % more. Roughly two-thirds of what the non-borrowers give up turns up in the borrowers' hands; the remaining third is a machine nobody buys, because it now costs more than the household next in line will pay.

## What the non-borrower does instead

| | change |
|---|---|
| Money in their account | **+4.3 %** |
| What they spend each month | +0.2 % |
| Number of things they buy | ±0.0 % (55.17 a year, both runs) |
| Average quality of those things | +0.5 % |

The money not spent on the washing machine does not go anywhere else. It sits there. They buy about the same number of things, very slightly better on average — because the purchases they lost were the big ones.

## Nobody is queuing

Worth saying plainly, because it is easy to picture this the wrong way: **no household in this town ever fails to buy something because the shop had run out.** Not once, in any month, in either run.

Everything that goes unbought goes unbought because it costs too much. The exclusion is a price, never a queue. There is no shortage — there is an auction, and some people are outbid every month.

## Conclusion

**The claim holds, and it is sharper than it was.** A household that never borrows a cent waits three years longer for a washing machine — seventeen instead of fourteen — and gets barely a third of the large appliances it wanted, because other households financed theirs. Its income never changed. It was simply outbid, month after month.

**But "buying on credit makes things more expensive" is still the wrong sentence.** Overall prices move by a tenth of a per cent. What credit does is *sort*: the things you can finance get sharply dearer and much harder to reach, the things you cannot get slightly cheaper and slightly easier. It is not inflation. It is a transfer, and it has a direction — from the people who do not finance, to the people who do.

**And the coat is the proof that it is credit doing it.** Two nearly identical purchases, opposite outcomes, one difference between them: whether you are offered the option to pay in instalments.

### What this cannot tell you

Any number above should be read with these attached.

**The two that make the figures look too small:**
- There is no second-hand market, so the cash buyer has no cheaper alternative to escape to.
- Manufacturers cannot change what they offer. In reality, more financed premium buying pulls production up-market, which would make the squeeze worse.

**The three that make them look too big:**
- **Supply cannot respond.** This is the big one. Shops here produce a fixed number of washing machines whatever the price does. In reality some of that price rise would be met by simply making more. The price effects above are therefore a **ceiling**, not an estimate.
- Incomes cannot respond either. There are no employers, no wages, no profits.
- Nobody ever fails to repay. Credit in this town has all of its effect on demand and none of its consequences.

**And one that is simply a limit of the exercise:** the prices, lifetimes and how much people want things were all chosen by hand. They were chosen so that anyone can check them against a shop window, and they were *not* adjusted afterwards to make the result look better — but they are still a set of assumptions, not measurements.

One more, in fairness. Whether a good can be bought on credit was assigned roughly according to how expensive it is, so "the harm follows size" and "the harm follows financing" are hard to tell apart in this table. The winter coat is the one row that separates them, and it is carrying more of the argument than a single row ideally should. A version of the town with two or three more large goods that cannot be financed would settle it properly. That has not been run.
